A network is a right-of-way that happens to carry light.

The glass is cheap. What costs money and takes years is the permission to bury it, the conduit it sits in, the beach where a cable comes ashore, and the license to transmit through the air. Telecom is a real estate and permitting business with a physics problem attached, and the AI buildout has made its slowest, dullest assets scarce again.

01  /  What it is

What a network business actually is

Four assets with four different lifespans, financed up front and paid back monthly.

A network company owns some combination of four things. Rights-of-way are the legal permission to occupy a strip of land: along a railroad, a highway, a pipeline, a utility pole line, or under a city street. Conduit and cable is the physical plant: plastic ducts in the ground and the fiber strands pulled through them. Electronics (transceivers, amplifiers, routers) light the fiber and are replaced every five to seven years as each generation carries more. Spectrum licenses let wireless carriers transmit on specific frequencies in specific places.

These assets age at very different speeds. A right-of-way can last a century. A conduit lasts decades and can hold several generations of cable. Fiber itself lasts twenty-five years or more. Electronics are obsolete within a decade. That spread is why the industry's economics are confusing from the outside: the expensive, slow-to-build part (trenching and permitting) is the part that barely changes, and the part that improves fastest (the optics) is a modest share of total cost.

The money arrives in the opposite shape from the spending. Construction is front-loaded, often for years before the first customer is lit. Revenue is recurring and slow, through monthly service fees or long-term contracts. That makes networks natural infrastructure assets for patient capital, and dangerous for anyone who builds on a demand forecast that arrives late. The dot-com era is the warning: carriers laid far more fiber than the market needed in 1998 to 2001, and Global Crossing, WorldCom and others went bankrupt, leaving dark fiber that took a decade to absorb.

02  /  The physics

Light in glass is slower than you think and faster than anything else

Attenuation sets how far a signal travels; dispersion and amplification set how much it can carry; the refractive index sets how long it takes.

Modern long-distance networks use single-mode fiber: a glass core about nine microns across, surrounded by cladding that keeps light bouncing inside. Signals travel in the infrared, mostly around 1,550 nanometers, where the glass absorbs least, losing about 0.2 decibels per kilometer. Every 60 to 100 kilometers an optical amplifier (usually an erbium-doped fiber amplifier) boosts the light without converting it to electricity. That is why a transatlantic cable needs powered repeaters on the seabed, fed by thousands of volts of direct current from the shore.

Capacity comes from wavelength-division multiplexing. A single fiber pair carries dozens of channels at different colors, and modern coherent optics encode many bits per symbol on each. A long-haul fiber pair in 2026 can carry on the order of tens of terabits per second, and upgrading the optics at each end raises that again without touching the glass. That is the deep reason old routes keep their value: the cable is a platform for future electronics.

Latency is the one thing upgrades cannot fix. Light in glass travels at roughly two-thirds of its speed in a vacuum, so every kilometer of route adds about 4.9 microseconds one way. Routes are never straight; they follow coastlines, railroads and permitted corridors. Hollow-core fiber, which guides light through air, cuts delay by about 30% and has moved from labs into production routes, led by Microsoft after its 2022 purchase of Lumenisity.

03  /  What is for sale

Seven things a carrier can sell you

The higher up the list, the more the carrier operates and the less the customer controls.

Network products form a ladder. At the bottom, the customer gets bare glass and does everything else; at the top, the carrier runs the whole service and the customer sees only an outcome. Hyperscalers have moved steadily down the ladder, buying raw fiber and lighting it with their own equipment, because at their scale owning the optics is cheaper and gives them control over capacity and routing. Enterprises mostly buy near the top.

04  /  The signature contract

An IRU is a lease that wants to be a sale

The indefeasible right of use lets a buyer pay up front for decades of exclusive use, without owning the cable.

An indefeasible right of use (IRU) grants the exclusive, irrevocable use of specific fibers or capacity for a long term, typically fifteen to twenty-five years. The buyer pays most of the price up front and then an annual fee for operations and maintenance, usually priced per route mile. The seller keeps legal title, maintains the route, and repairs cuts. Economically the buyer owns the strands; legally it rents them.

That hybrid nature is why IRUs have an accounting history. Depending on the terms, a seller may recognize revenue over the life of the contract or, in some structures, treat the deal as a sales-type lease and recognize much of it at signing. In 2001 and 2002, carriers including Global Crossing were found to have swapped IRUs with each other and booked the incoming payments as revenue while treating the outgoing ones as capital spending, inflating revenue on deals with little economic substance. Investors still read IRU revenue recognition footnotes carefully.

05  /  The new demand

AI made long-haul routes scarce again

Glass is abundant. What ran short is permitted routes and spare conduit into power-rich sites, because training and inference campuses sit where power is, not where networks are.

For most of the 2010s, long-haul fiber in the United States was a mature, somewhat oversupplied business. Two things changed that. First, AI campuses are sited for power and land, which puts them in places like rural Texas, Ohio, Louisiana or the Mountain West rather than beside the traditional internet exchanges. Second, large training and inference systems increasingly span several buildings or campuses, and moving model weights, checkpoints and training traffic between them takes bulk dark fiber, not a few wavelengths.

The order sizes changed accordingly. Zayo's product chief told Fierce that its historical long-haul orders averaged 8 to 12 fibers, and Windstream Wholesale reported customers asking for as many as 864. Zayo committed in January 2025 to build 5,000 miles of new long-haul routes and later described an additional anchor deal adding about 8,000 route miles of new routes and overbuilds. In July 2026, Verizon disclosed a Google contract worth more than $1 billion to connect Google's data centers with dark fiber and said more hyperscaler deals were expected.

New long-haul routes are slow. Permits along railroads, highways and federal land, plus environmental review and construction seasons, mean eighteen months to three years for a significant route. That is why existing routes with spare conduit, and companies holding them, became valuable quickly: the cheapest way to add capacity is to pull new cable through a duct that already exists.

Read with care

"AI-driven pipeline" figures in carrier announcements mix signed contracts, verbal commitments and opportunities. Only signed revenue under contract should be compared with construction capital. Section 11 lists the other ways fiber numbers are presented flatteringly.

06  /  Homes and businesses

The last mile is where the money and the politics are

Fiber, cable, fixed wireless and satellite compete for the same household, with different cost curves.

Connecting a home is the most expensive per-customer part of any network, because each passing requires digging or pole work for a single bill. Four technologies compete. Fiber to the home has the highest capacity and the longest life but the highest build cost, most of it in civil works rather than glass. Cable (DOCSIS over coaxial) reuses an existing plant and upgrades incrementally. Fixed wireless access uses spare mobile network capacity to serve homes from cell towers; in the US, T-Mobile and Verizon built it into a large business in a few years. Satellite, transformed by low-earth-orbit constellations such as Starlink, serves places where wires will never pay.

The economics push toward consolidation, because fiber builders need scale to buy equipment and spread overhead, and because owning both fiber and wireless lets a carrier bundle. Verizon agreed in 2024 to buy Frontier for about $20 billion, with closing expected in 2026. AT&T agreed in 2025 to buy Lumen's consumer fiber business for about $5.75 billion. Public subsidy follows the same map: the US BEAD program, about $42 billion for unserved areas, was restructured in 2025 to be more technology-neutral, shifting some awards from fiber toward satellite and fixed wireless.

07  /  Under the ocean

Almost all intercontinental data rides on cables the width of a garden hose

Subsea systems carry the overwhelming majority of international traffic. They are owned by consortia and, increasingly, by the largest cloud companies.

A subsea cable is a bundle of fiber pairs, typically a dozen or more on new systems, wrapped in copper for power, a steel strength member, and armour near shore. Repeaters every 50 to 100 kilometers amplify the light, powered by a high-voltage DC line from landing stations at each end. In deep water the cable lies on the seabed; near shore it is buried to protect it from anchors and fishing gear.

Ownership has shifted. Historically, cables were built by consortia of national telecom carriers, each owning capacity. Since the mid-2010s, Google, Meta, Microsoft and Amazon have become major investors and sometimes sole owners of new systems, because their traffic is the largest on most routes. Meta announced Project Waterworth in 2025, a planned system of roughly 50,000 kilometers. Owning the cable gives a cloud company capacity at cost and the ability to route around competitors and chokepoints.

Cables break often, mostly by accident. The International Cable Protection Committee attributes nearly 30% of faults to dragged anchors, with fishing gear also a leading cause. Repairs need specialized ships, cost roughly $1 million to $3 million each, and depend on permits in the waters concerned. The repair fleet is small and aging, and industry groups spent 2026 lobbying governments to fund new vessels.

08  /  Renting altitude

A tower company rents altitude

Build a structure once, sign several carriers to hang antennas on it, and raise the rent every year.

A tower company owns or leases the land and the steel and rents vertical space to wireless carriers. The first tenant roughly covers the cost; each additional tenant is nearly pure margin, because the structure, ground lease and maintenance are shared. US tower leases typically carry fixed annual escalators of around 3%, and amendments when a carrier adds equipment raise rent further. The key operating metric is the tenancy ratio, tenants per tower.

The three large US tower companies, American Tower, Crown Castle and SBA Communications, are structured as real estate investment trusts. Their risks are carrier consolidation, which removes duplicate antennas (the T-Mobile and Sprint merger led to years of decommissioning), and carrier exits. In 2025 EchoStar agreed to sell large spectrum holdings to AT&T and SpaceX, effectively ending its plan to run a fourth national network, which reduced expected tower demand. Crown Castle agreed in 2025 to sell its fiber and small-cell businesses to Zayo and EQT to become a pure tower company, a sign that investors pay more for the simpler model.

09  /  Invisible land

Spectrum is land you cannot see, sold at auction

Low frequencies travel far, high frequencies carry more, and the middle is where the money is.

Radio spectrum is divided into bands, and each behaves differently. Low-band spectrum (below 1 GHz) travels far and penetrates buildings but carries little data. Mid-band (roughly 1 to 7 GHz) balances coverage and capacity and is where 5G earns its money. High-band millimeter wave carries enormous amounts of data over very short distances. Governments license bands to carriers, increasingly by auction, and the licenses become some of the largest assets on carriers' balance sheets.

The US auction record shows the price of mid-band. The 2020 to 2021 lower C-band auction raised more than $80 billion, and satellite operators received about $9.7 billion to clear the band quickly. The FCC's general auction authority then lapsed in March 2023 and stayed dormant for about two years until the 2025 budget reconciliation law, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, restored it and set a pipeline of 800 MHz to be auctioned by 2034. On 22 July 2026 the FCC voted to auction 160 MHz of upper C-band (3.98 to 4.14 GHz) through 3,248 licenses, 60% more than the minimum Congress required, with a deadline of July 2027. Combined with the lower C-band, it creates a contiguous 440 MHz block. Aviation altimeter interference, the dispute that delayed lower C-band deployment, was handled through safeguards the FAA endorsed and rebates for equipment retrofits paid by winning bidders.

An auction is not a network

Build-out follows an auction by years. Satellite operators must move their services, carriers must buy and install radios, and towers must be amended. The FCC's own plan targeted lighting the upper C-band for most Americans before the end of 2030.

10  /  Who pays for it

Why fiber trades like infrastructure and telcos trade like utilities with debt

Contracted, long-lived cash flows attract infrastructure funds; capital-hungry, competitive retail businesses do not.

Investors split network businesses into two kinds. Infrastructure-like assets have long contracts, low churn and inflation escalators: towers, dark fiber leased to hyperscalers, and data center interconnection. These attract infrastructure funds and pension capital and are financed with long-term debt, often securitized against the contracted cash flows; tower and fiber asset-backed securities are an established market. Retail telecom (consumer broadband and mobile) has more competition, churn and continuous capital spending, and trades at lower valuations.

The ownership map reflects that split. Zayo was taken private in 2020 by DigitalBridge and EQT for about $14.3 billion. Lumen sold its consumer fiber business to AT&T to focus on enterprise and hyperscaler fiber. Infrastructure funds own large stakes in tower portfolios and metro fiber platforms worldwide. The same lenders and structures that fund data centers fund fiber, which ties this guide to the capital flows mapped in The Circular Economy of AI Capital.

11  /  Who decides

The rules: licenses, landings, poles and politics

Most network delays are regulatory or civil, not technical.

In the United States the FCC regulates interstate communications, licenses spectrum and grants cable landing licenses under the Submarine Cable Landing Act of 1921. Landing applications are reviewed by an interagency group known as Team Telecom (the Justice, Homeland Security and Defense departments) for national security risk, and in 2025 the FCC adopted rules to restrict licenses for cables involving foreign adversary countries. State public utility commissions and municipalities control most rights-of-way, permits and pole attachments, which is where local build-outs are won or lost. Net neutrality rules adopted by the FCC in 2024 were struck down by the Sixth Circuit in January 2025.

Elsewhere, the European Union treats subsea cable security as a policy priority after the Baltic incidents, and many countries require local approvals for every repair inside their territorial waters, which is why repairs in politically sensitive areas take months rather than weeks.

12  /  Red flags

The promotional playbook

Six ways network assets get described more generously than they deserve.

Units

Route miles versus fiber miles

A 100-mile route with 144 fibers is 14,400 fiber miles. Mixing the two inflates footprint claims by two orders of magnitude.

Revenue

IRU revenue up front

Check whether long-term contracts are recognized at signing or over their life. Up-front recognition flatters growth and is not repeatable.

Pipeline

"AI deals" that are not contracts

Pipeline, verbal awards and signed contracts are reported together. Ask for contracted revenue and its term.

Coverage

Passings versus customers

A home "passed" by fiber is not a customer. Penetration rate, the share of passings that pay, is the number that funds the build.

Towers

Tenancy ratios without churn

Growth from new tenants can be offset by decommissioning after carrier mergers. Read net, not gross, leasing.

History

The dot-com rhyme

The last fiber boom ended with bankruptcies and a decade of dark fiber. Watch for speculative routes built without anchor tenants.

14  /  The current position

Where networks stand in October 2026

Hyperscaler fiber deals, a spectrum pipeline reopened, and subsea routes treated as security assets.

Data as of 15 October 2026. Figures and events in this section were checked against the sources listed at the end of the page on or before that date. Treat this section as a dated snapshot; the explanatory sections are evergreen.

Three trends define the year. First, fiber for AI is now a contracted business rather than a forecast. Verizon's Google dark-fiber deal, disclosed with its July results alongside Google's raised 2026 capital spending guidance of $195 billion to $205 billion, followed earlier multi-billion-dollar programs at Lumen and Zayo. The scarce input is not glass but existing conduit and permitted routes into power-rich sites.

Second, spectrum is moving again. With auction authority restored, the FCC adopted upper C-band rules in July 2026 for an auction by July 2027, with incentive payments to satellite operators and rebates for aviation retrofits. Further auctions, including the 2.7 GHz band, are planned toward 2028.

Third, subsea cables are security infrastructure. Cuts in the Red Sea in 2024 and 2025, repeated Baltic incidents including January 2026, and conflict near the Strait of Hormuz have pushed governments toward patrols, repair-ship funding and route diversity. New systems are being routed to avoid single chokepoints, which raises their cost and length and therefore their latency.

15  /  The words, defined

The vocabulary

Filter by category or search. Network vocabulary mixes physics, real estate and accounting; several words (C-band, layer, capacity) mean different things in different parts of the business.

Single-mode fiber Physics & optics

Fiber with a core about nine microns across carrying one light path; the standard for long distances.

Multimode fiber Physics & optics

Fiber with a wider core carrying many light paths; cheaper optics, short distances, mostly inside buildings.

Core and cladding Physics & optics

The light-carrying glass center of a fiber and the lower-index glass around it that keeps light inside.

Refractive index Physics & optics

How much a medium slows light; about 1.47 for fiber glass, which sets latency at roughly 4.9 µs per km.

Attenuation Physics & optics

Loss of signal strength along a fiber, about 0.2 dB per km at 1,550 nm.

Decibel Physics & optics

A logarithmic unit of signal ratio; every 3 dB of loss halves the power.

Optical C-band Physics & optics

The 1,530 to 1,565 nm wavelength window where fiber loss is lowest and amplifiers work best. Unrelated to the radio C-band.

Dispersion Physics & optics

Spreading of light pulses over distance, which limits data rates unless compensated, now mostly in digital signal processing.

EDFA Physics & optics

Erbium-doped fiber amplifier, boosting light directly without converting it to electricity.

Raman amplification Physics & optics

Amplification using the fiber itself as the gain medium, extending reach on long spans.

Regenerator Physics & optics

Equipment that converts light to electricity and back to clean up a degraded signal.

DWDM Physics & optics

Dense wavelength-division multiplexing: many optical channels at different wavelengths sharing one fiber.

Coherent optics Physics & optics

Transmission encoding data in phase and amplitude of light, with digital processing at the receiver.

Spectral efficiency Physics & optics

Bits per second carried per hertz of optical spectrum; what each optics generation improves.

Transponder Physics & optics

The device converting client traffic onto a wavelength, and back, at each end of a route.

ROADM Physics & optics

Reconfigurable optical add-drop multiplexer, which routes wavelengths through a network without conversion to electronics.

Fiber pair Physics & optics

Two fibers, one for each direction; the unit of capacity on subsea and long-haul systems.

Hollow-core fiber Physics & optics

Fiber guiding light through air, cutting latency by about 30%.

Latency Physics & optics

The time for data to travel between points.

Splice Physics & optics

A permanent joint between two fibers, usually fused with an electric arc.

OTDR Physics & optics

Optical time-domain reflectometer, which locates breaks and losses along a fiber by timing reflections.

Installed fiber with no electronics; the buyer lights it.

Lit service Products

Capacity sold with the carrier operating the electronics.

Wavelength service Products

A dedicated optical channel, such as 100G, 400G or 800G, between two points.

Spectrum service Products

A slice of optical spectrum on a carrier's line system, lit with the buyer's own transponders.

Ethernet private line Products

A point-to-point private connection at a fixed bandwidth.

IP transit Products

Paid access to the entire internet through a carrier's network.

Dedicated internet access Products

An uncontended business internet connection with a guaranteed rate.

Software-defined wide area networking, which routes enterprise traffic across multiple connection types.
